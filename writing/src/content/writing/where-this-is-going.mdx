I've spent the better part of the last three years thinking about how AI could transform creative work. Three years ago, if pressed, I would have predicted that specialized generative diffusion models (e.g. Seedance 2.5, Midjourney, Nano Banana) would gradually replace rendering and compositing pipelines, hollowing out studios as image and video generation improved.

However, the more I studied these models, the less plausible any of that seemed. They are marvels of engineering but simultaneously narrowly capable. It would require a leap in capability of uncertain magnitude to transform them into general-purpose creative workers that could be applied to workflows outside their medium. (And by the time such a leap arrived, we would likely be dealing with something else entirely).

In December 2025, programmers everywhere experienced a rubicon in their careers when agents like Claude began to actually get good enough at coding to be usable professionally. For visual effects artists, 3D artists, and other creatives, **it is December 2025 right now.** I believe the collective experience of programmers could offer some insight into what this transition will look like for us.

It is worth being precise about the threshold I am referring to: General-purpose large language models like OpenAI's [GPT-6 Astra](https://openai.com/index/gpt-6-astra/) and Anthropic's [Claude Opus 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-opus-5-5) have learned to use complex visual effects software (like Nuke, Blender, or Houdini) and can be used to execute real work in that software on behalf of humans--at a level comparable to and increasingly exceeding that of skilled humans--with the only input being natural language instructions.

I would also like to say that these capabilities have a hard limit. Given a well defined task and legible goal, an agent is enormously capable; given a vague ambition, it produces something vague, average, or--what I prefer to call--**stochastically probable**.

As artists, our thinking, taste and the years it takes to learn this craft stay with us, and anyone who uses these tools to skip that learning will produce work that shows it. There is a hard line between being able to use complex visual effects software and knowing what to make in that software (or whether the output even looks good).

I liken this to the difference between understanding law as it is written versus understanding law as a practice.

From what I have observed, it is the repetitive labor that sits between an artist and the craft, such as UV unwrapping, managing assets and designing AOVs where agents can be leveraged the most, but as capabilities continue to grow, I expect to see big improvements to these models' ability to produce compelling visuals when directed well. That is already possible with a lot of patience and effort, as in the example below, where I directed an agent to build and render a scene from scratch in Houdini:

## What happened to programming

A few years ago I began programming again as a hobby, mostly Python (for making custom tools in Nuke), and HTML/Javascript because I needed to build the first Biohazard website myself. It is because of this that I found myself immersed in niche developer communities and involved in conversations about AI.

By mid-2025, AI agents from the likes of Cursor and Anthropic were everywhere. In order to execute any real tasks with these agents, you had to write [enormous specifications](https://docs.bmad-method.org/), engineer prompts very carefully and perform a kind of harness engineering to overcome their shortcomings. To put it plainly: they weren't very good for anything other than [vibe-coding](https://x.com/karpathy/status/1886192184808149383?a=gD9liPPRnKr-GSf1ei0&s=20). However, when [Opus 4.5](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-4-5) came out at the end of November 2025, this changed. This model could be given a task and would simply do it. I could say, "Make these changes and show me when you're done," and the changes would work--or, when they didn't, the model would return and fix them. And as such, "[agentic engineering](https://aiagentssimplified.substack.com/p/from-vibe-coding-to-agentic-engineering)" was born.

Like many other developers I knew, I was immediately hooked. I even began a large software project called Signet, and within a few months, had a pseudo-factory of agents working on it around the clock, merging [nearly a thousand pull requests](https://github.com/Signet-AI/signetai) within a very short period of time.

This pattern repeated across the industry. Programmers everywhere saw the models [become genuinely capable](https://www.businessinsider.com/andrej-karpathy-claude-code-manual-skills-atrophy-software-engineering-tesla-2026-1). Today, at Anthropic, Claude writes [more than 90% of the code](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/technology/tech-news/claude-ai-now-writes-over-90-of-the-code-at-anthropic-company-in-its-ipo-filing-says-the-job-of-an-engineer-at-anthropic-is-now-to/articleshow/135231913.cms). OpenAI's Codex lead says [most of Codex is built by Codex](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2025/12/how-openai-is-using-gpt-5-codex-to-improve-the-ai-tool-itself/). Spotify reports that its best developers [haven't written a line of code since December](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/12/spotify-says-its-best-developers-havent-written-a-line-of-code-since-december-thanks-to-ai); DoorDash puts AI-written code at [about two-thirds](https://www.businessinsider.com/doordash-ceo-tony-xu-ai-helping-engineers-workforce-priority-customers-2026-5). One engineer at SpaceXAI shipped [more than two thousand pull requests in a single month](https://x.ai/bot/guides/grok-bot-for-engineering) with her agents. Anthropic is merging code so quickly that it had to [build its own version control system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control), because git could not keep up. These are not marginal improvements. They represent a step change in what a single human can accomplish with AI assistance, and they arrived with stunning speed.

## The same shift in visual effects

The same transition has already begun in visual effects and 3D, over the past year at Biohazard, we've delegated the management of our internal asset library, server infrastructure and render farm entirely to agents (a responsibility that previously fell on my shoulders, mostly). These agents run on our own systems and (when powered by the latest models from OpenAI and Anthropic) can now [drive Nuke, Houdini, and Blender](https://www.vp-land.com/stories/ai-agents-move-into-houdini-unreal-and-adobe-as-nvidia-rallies-creative-apps-around-mcp) well enough to assist on tasks that are well-scoped and easily verifiable, like matchmove, layout and roto.

On one of our most recent projects, I personally handled the build of an entire city as well as the subsequent FX, lighting, rendering, and compositing on the shots that took place within that environment. Much of this process, however, was augmented with about fifteen separate agents running at once, each with a separate task.

Under my direction, agents built the city procedurally from OpenStreetMap data and LiDAR scans, set up the FX simulations (from the rig built by our FX artist), designed AOVs in Mantra and Cycles, and managed sending shots to the render farm and getting them back using custom tooling built on top of the Fox Renderfarm SDK.

> The high-level technical decisions and creative direction on those shots, such as the look of the city to the timing of the lightning, were made by people. Nothing on screen was left up to the judgment of an LLM or lifted from its training data. Our project files lived on our own server and the agents, as well as their memory and custom tools, ran on machines that Biohazard owns. Every model provider worked under zero data retention, and nothing sensitive was stored or used for training.

## Future impact and some predictions

The implications of this change on our industry and art-form are difficult to overstate. Some impacts will be positive but there will be negative changes as well.

Some good news first: The amount of work a single artist can accomplish is about to increase dramatically. We will see individuals capable of producing entire short films, music videos and eventually features using software they already know. With AI systems at their side, a music video that might have taken a dozen artists two years ago could perhaps be made today by three or four with access to the right agents.

However, a downside is that AI is already being used to [proliferate slop at machine speed](https://www.tomsguide.com/ai/the-internet-is-dying-researchers-uncovered-200-fake-ai-websites-youve-likely-visited). Slop meaning content that is [lacking in effort, quality, or meaning, and usually produced in high volume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AI_slop). This is a disease that rots our minds, communities, standards and our work.

I have a few predictions for what we may see in our industry as models continue to get better:

- **The gradual death of outsourcing:** Visual effects companies may hire fewer artists, in favor of giving each member of a team more responsibility/more shots, and more projects will be done in-house.
- **Lightweight, ephemeral teams:** For many short form projects, studios as a whole may get bypassed entirely in favor of one-off teams of freelancers hired directly by an agency or client.
- **Communication becomes a bottleneck:** The output of individual artists will likely eclipse what teams [can efficiently parallelize](https://herbsleb.org/web-pubs/pdfs/Herbsleb-Empirical-2003.pdf). Agents can often parallelize work [better than a team would](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.20702). Parallel work becomes a matter of opening another thread. More and more, one artist will be able to carry an entire commercial or video alone.

## Ethics, capitalism, etc.

In many ways, AI is a tool that allows the wealthy access to skills [without having to hire humans for access to those skills](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0160791X24002677). So it is easy to imagine why an artist (or anyone, for that matter) would be apprehensive about using AI in the manner I have described above.

Additionally, many artists I know have had their work stolen for the training of AI models, and I'm not about to ask or pressure them to pay for access to a distilled version of that data. However, a wise man once said *"you can't un-fuck the thanksgiving turkey"*. So while we should be having conversations about copyright, responsible AI usage, local models, and ethically sourced datasets, I believe it's smart to accept that AI is here--that the blast radius from its impact grows larger every day. It's [well-funded](https://finance.yahoo.com/technology/ai/articles/inside-anthropics-massive-wild-ipo-225844408.html) (Anthropic alone has committed almost $650 billion to computing), and it's going to take years for regulations that protect workers to be put in place, and even that is not guaranteed.

In the same way that using AI doesn't automatically make a project better, I believe AI usage doesn't automatically make a project worse. I think that if there is such a thing as acceptable AI usage, what sets the thin gray line between it and AI slop is measured judgment and the granular details of how that AI was used.

Plainly: If AI is the wrong tool for a shot or a project, it shouldn't be used. A production with a no-AI policy should receive exactly that, and every client should know how their work was made.

The environmental cost matters as well. Google measured a median text prompt at [0.24 watt-hours](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/infrastructure/measuring-the-environmental-impact-of-ai-inference), and a single render node with an RTX 5090 draws upwards of 600 watts under load, the equivalent of about 2,500 of those prompts every hour. That comparison flatters AI, though: an agent working through a task makes hundreds of far larger calls, and none of it counts the energy spent training these models or the data centers being built to serve them.

## The cost of rented intelligence

Ultimately, this will be **uncomfortable** and I expect many artists and workers to leave the visual effects industry because of it. From the outside, the next year will probably look like a mass devaluation of labor: As models get better the work may no longer be as expensive or as time-consuming to do.

Furthermore: the cost of making a visual effects shot could fall toward nothing. Programming again offers the closest preview: code is more or less free now, and as such, traditionally trained programmers who craft beautiful code by hand are [no longer the most sought-after group](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/11/ai-software-developer-jobs.html); the most valuable are [product minded individuals](https://www.businessinsider.com/forward-deployed-engineer-jobs-in-demand-2026-5) who can understand the needs of an organization's users and orchestrate agents to build entire applications in a night. I predict that the same dynamic is coming for creative work.

There is a deeper and more troubling dimension to this. To do our best work and earn a living, we will increasingly be renting intelligence from external providers. We will pay for access and hope that the cost remains bearable, but just imagine not being able to complete your project on time because Anthropic servers are unavailable or the cost of the Claude Max plan increased.

The more serious version of this concern is about thinking itself. If you hand a model every decision, you stop developing the judgment that made you worth hiring in the first place.

## Why now

For years I expected the change to come from a model that makes pictures. What arrived is a model that uses our tools. I think that is the better outcome for artists, because the tools, workflows and pipeline are still ours, and the people who know them best will get the most out of what comes next, provided they start now.

Now is the time for artists to learn these tools. You can choose not to, and I understand the reluctance, but if you wish to keep working in this field, you may end up competing against artists who have embraced these capabilities.

I do not think this is the death of the industry. I am, if anything, optimistic about its long-term possibilities. The outcome is not preordained, but the transition will be turbulent for all; for some it will amount to the end of their visual effects journey, for others it may be the start of theirs.
