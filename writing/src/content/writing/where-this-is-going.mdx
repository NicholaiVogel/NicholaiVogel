I have spent years thinking about how AI would transform creative work. Two years ago, if pressed, I would have predicted that specialized generative models—diffusion models, in particular—would gradually replace the rendering and compositing pipeline, hollowing out studios as image and video generation improved. But the more I studied these models, the less plausible that story seemed. They are marvels of engineering, but they are narrow. Transforming them into general-purpose creative workers would require a capability leap of uncertain magnitude, and by the time such a leap arrived, we would likely be dealing with something else entirely. What actually happened is both simpler and more unsettling: General purpose large language models learned to use our software, just by writing code. Programmers went through this first, and for them the turning point was December 2025. For visual effects artists, 3D artists, and other creatives, it is December 2025 right now.

It is worth being precise about the threshold I am referring to. It is not the point at which a model can generate a convincing image, but the point at which it can operate the same tools a professional uses—like Nuke, Blender or Houdini—at a level *comparable* to, and increasingly *exceeding*, that of skilled humans.

## What happened to programming

I watched this threshold arrive in programming first. A few years ago, I began programming again as a hobby, mostly Python for custom Nuke tools and whatever JavaScript I needed to build the first Biohazard website myself. Through mid-2025, AI coding tools like Cursor's autocomplete were impressive, but agents were difficult to steer; you had to write enormous specifications and perform a kind of harness engineering just to get them to execute a real task. This changed when [Opus 4.5](https://www.anthropic.com/news/claude-opus-4-5) came out. It could be given a task and would simply do it. I could say, "Make these changes and show me when you're done," and the changes would work—or, when they didn't, the model would return and fix them. I began a larger project called Signet, and within a few months, I had a factory of agents working on it around the clock and had merged [nearly a thousand pull requests](https://github.com/Signet-AI/signetai).

This pattern is now repeating across the industry. Around December 2025, programmers everywhere saw the models [become genuinely capable](https://www.businessinsider.com/andrej-karpathy-claude-code-manual-skills-atrophy-software-engineering-tesla-2026-1). At Anthropic, Claude now writes [more than 90% of the code](https://timesofindia.indiatimes.com/technology/tech-news/claude-ai-now-writes-over-90-of-the-code-at-anthropic-company-in-its-ipo-filing-says-the-job-of-an-engineer-at-anthropic-is-now-to/articleshow/135231913.cms). OpenAI's Codex lead says [most of Codex is built by Codex](https://arstechnica.com/ai/2025/12/how-openai-is-using-gpt-5-codex-to-improve-the-ai-tool-itself/). Spotify reports that its best developers [haven't written a line of code since December](https://techcrunch.com/2026/02/12/spotify-says-its-best-developers-havent-written-a-line-of-code-since-december-thanks-to-ai); DoorDash puts AI-written code at [about two-thirds](https://www.businessinsider.com/doordash-ceo-tony-xu-ai-helping-engineers-workforce-priority-customers-2026-5). One engineer at SpaceXAI shipped [more than two thousand pull requests in a single month](https://x.ai/bot/guides/grok-bot-for-engineering) with her agents. Anthropic is merging code so quickly that it had to [build its own version control system](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/redesigning-source-control), because git could not keep up. These are not marginal improvements. They represent a step change in what a single human can accomplish with AI assistance, and they arrived with stunning speed.

## The same shift in visual effects

The same transition has already begun in visual effects and 3D art, with the introduction of models like OpenAI's [GPT-6 Astra](https://openai.com/index/gpt-6-astra/) and Anthropic's [Claude Opus 5.5](https://www.anthropic.com/claude-opus-5-5). Over the past year at Biohazard, we delegated the management of our internal asset library, server infrastructure, render farm and pipeline entirely to agents. When powered by the latest models, these same agents can now [drive Nuke, Houdini, and Blender](https://www.vp-land.com/stories/ai-agents-move-into-houdini-unreal-and-adobe-as-nvidia-rallies-creative-apps-around-mcp)—not perfectly yet, but well enough to do real production work. In some cases, they are already better than junior artists, especially in areas that are mechanically verifiable, like matchmove and roto. Agents are quietly absorbing the workloads of entire departments.

## Twenty-three shots

I can say this with some confidence because I have done it. I am a VFX supervisor, but I am still an artist; on all of our projects I am hands-on, doing shots alongside everyone else, because that is how this industry works when deadlines are tight. On a recent project, I personally handled the build of an entire city and the lightning effects on most of the city shots, working from our FX artist's rig. End to end, that was me directing my agents, with about fifteen sessions running at once at the peak. The agents built the city procedurally, set up the effects, and ran the render farm; one of them wrote its own tooling on top of the Fox Renderfarm SDK to move renders to and from the farm without blowing up the budget. It did the job of a render wrangler. I would estimate that a team would have taken four times as long. This was a real client job—twenty-three shots, with a crew of outside artists working in the same pipeline as my agents. Other artists on the crew worked through the same agents, asking them for generations, renders and file deliveries directly. Work at that scale is possible today, with the software we already use.

## Smaller teams, cheaper work

The implications are difficult to overstate. The amount of work a single artist can accomplish is about to increase dramatically—and already has. We will see individuals capable of producing entire short films or music videos, and eventually features, using software they already know, with AI systems at their side. A music video that might have cost $110,000 two years ago could perhaps be made today for $20,000, in a fraction of the time, by two or three people with access to the right agents.

<WipeCompare beforeLabel="Raw render" before={sxrs016Raw} after={sxrs016Final} alt="SXRS shot 016, the Ben Franklin Bridge" caption="Shot 016. Drag to wipe between the raw beauty render and the final frame." />

As this continues, studios will hire fewer artists who each do more. And in many cases, studios will be bypassed entirely in favor of project-specific bootstrap teams of freelancers, empowered by these tools. Mid-level studios should be particularly concerned: they are large enough to have substantial fixed costs, yet not so large that they are protected by a parent company, and the output of individual artists is about to eclipse what they can efficiently coordinate. They can adopt these tools too, of course, but the bottleneck will shift to [communication and direction](https://herbsleb.org/web-pubs/pdfs/Herbsleb-Empirical-2003.pdf). When I work with agents, I already move faster than most people can follow, and when I need parallel work done, agents do it [better than a team would](https://doi.org/10.1287/orsc.2025.20702). Parallel work becomes a matter of opening another thread. More and more, one artist will be able to carry an entire commercial or video alone.

## The cost of renting intelligence

This will be **uncomfortable**, and I expect many people to leave the industry because of it. From the outside it will look like a mass devaluation of labor, because making the work will no longer be expensive. Furthermore, as AI improves, the cost of making a visual effects shot will fall toward nothing. Programming again offers the closest preview: code is more or less free now, and as such, traditionally trained programmers who craft beautiful code by hand are [no longer the most sought-after group](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/11/ai-software-developer-jobs.html); the most valuable are [product minded individuals](https://www.businessinsider.com/forward-deployed-engineer-jobs-in-demand-2026-5) who can understand the needs of an organization's users and orchestrate agents to build entire applications in a night. The same dynamic is coming for creative work, and I suspect it will be equally disruptive.

There is a deeper and more troubling dimension to this. To do our best work and earn a living, we will increasingly be renting intelligence from external providers. We will pay for access and hope that the cost remains bearable—and it already strains the economics of a small project. No one can spend a job's budget on [four Claude Max plans at $200 a month each](https://support.claude.com/en/articles/11049741-what-is-the-max-plan) and still make rent.

## Why now

I do not think this is the death of the industry. I am, if anything, optimistic about its long-term possibilities. But the transition will be turbulent for all; for some it will amount to the end of their visual effects journey. The outcome is not preordained. Now is the time for artists to learn these tools. You can choose not to, and I understand the reluctance to do so, but if you wish to keep working in this field, you will be competing against artists who have embraced these capabilities, and they will be so far ahead in speed and efficiency that traditional skill alone will not close the gap.

For years, I expected the pivotal change to be a model that makes pictures. It turned out to be a model that uses our tools. That is, on balance, better news for artists than what I feared, because the tools, workflows and pipeline remain ours, and those who know them best are best positioned to benefit from what is coming. But only if we begin now.
